# Inspiration — "Roy G. Biv"

## The found thing

The assessment suite colors every note by instrument family:

| family  | color     | which is…    |
|---------|-----------|--------------|
| voice   | `#e07a9e` | pink-red     |
| brass   | `#e76f51` | red-orange   |
| plucked | `#f4a261` | amber-yellow |
| winds   | `#2a9d8f` | green        |
| synth   | `#00b4d8` | cyan         |
| strings | `#457b9d` | indigo-blue  |
| keys    | `#9b5de5` | violet       |

Seven families. Stack them in that order, top to bottom, and read the
initials of the colors: **R, O, Y, G, B, I, V.** The toolkit's palette is a
rainbow that has been hiding in every roll this project ever rendered,
scattered and waiting to be sorted. That is the whole piece: *sort the
orchestra into a rainbow.* Nobody painted it there — the picture is what
correct orchestration looks like.

And a rainbow is already a musical object. It is an arch — the oldest
phrase shape there is, rise and fall — played seven times at once, in
parallel, each voice a fixed interval below the last. Musicians have a name
for seven lines locked in parallel thirds moving as one: **block harmony** —
the big-band shout chorus, the gospel choir on the final refrain. A rainbow
*is* a shout chorus. It even tells you its own voicing: stacked thirds,
about two octaves top to bottom, every vertical slice a thirteenth chord.

## The picture is the form

The drawing every kid makes after a storm — gray sky, rain, lightning, then
the rainbow and the sun — is also a complete emotional arc, and time already
flows left-to-right across the roll. So the scene *is* the form:

- **Gray sky** (opening): pale tremolo cloud-blobs, high and soft — low
  velocity literally renders them washed out. Minor key, brushes hissing
  underneath (invisible — see below).
- **Rain** (the scherzo): pizzicato strings. Strings print *blue* — the rain
  draws itself in the right color. Scattered falling two- and three-note
  drips, streaking down the left third of the canvas onto the ground line.
  Playful, not sad: rain you stomp in.
- **Lightning, then thunder** (the joke that proves the concept): lightning
  is a jagged amber zigzag — a violent down-up-down run, guitar and harp in
  octaves, fast enough to print as a bolt. Thunder is the drum kit and
  timpani detonating *a beat later* — and percussion never prints on the
  roll. You **see** the lightning; you only **hear** the thunder. Light
  arrives before sound, in the picture and in the score, because the medium
  itself refuses to draw thunder. That is the most honest raindrawing
  physics this piece could ask for.
- **The ground** (whole width): walking bass, low and steady, prints as an
  amber-sand line along the bottom of the frame. The horizon is the groove.
  Rain drips land on it; the rainbow's feet stand on it.
- **THE RAINBOW** (the shout chorus, the middle ~90 seconds): seven bands
  enter one by one from the outer arc inward — the fan opens — each tracing
  the same stepwise climb, a diatonic third apart, over the top and down the
  far side. Modulation lift at the crown. Every band is one instrument
  family, so the stripes come out in ROYGBIV order *for free*. Underneath,
  the invisible rhythm section cooks: this is a second-line strut, a
  when-the-saints jubilee, not a hymn.
- **The sun** (coda, top right): harp glissandi and plucked shimmer — amber
  again — swirled into a disc in the top corner. Sparkle tag, one last
  choir "ooh," done.

Velocity doubles as paint: the storm is translucent, the rainbow is
saturated (it is also the loudest tutti), the sun glitters mezzo. The
dynamic-arc panel under the roll should read as: drizzle → CRACK → strut →
blaze → glow.

## The band: Roy G. Biv and the Spectrum Seven

A seven-piece band where each chair *is* a stripe. Top to bottom of the
arch:

- **R** — choir "oohs" (voice, pink-red): the halo on top of the stack
- **O** — trumpet section (brass, red-orange): the lead, the preacher
- **Y** — guitar (plucked, amber): riff engine; doubles as lightning, bass, and sun duty (harp)
- **G** — clarinet (winds, green): the klezmer grin in the middle of the choir
- **B** — square-wave synth (synth, cyan): the cheeky kid who snuck into the band
- **I** — string section (strings, indigo): also the rain, pizzicato
- **V** — organ (keys, violet): gospel floor of the stack

Plus the invisible men: drum kit and timpani (thunder, groove) — the
rhythm section that never appears in the photo but without whom nothing
moves. A band this motley shouldn't work, which is why it's fun: sorted
into thirds and pointed at the same arch, seven families that never play
together *become a spectrum*.

## Why this is the right kind of entangled

Every image element decomposes into a musical necessity, and vice versa:

| in the picture        | in the music                                 |
|-----------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| rain dots             | pizzicato scherzo material                   |
| cloud blobs           | soft tremolo pads (the pale = quiet pun)     |
| lightning bolt        | the zigzag run that cues every thunder hit   |
| invisible thunder     | drums/timpani — groove with no visual cost   |
| ground line           | the walking bass that carries the whole form |
| seven stripes         | seven-part block harmony, one family each    |
| arch shape            | the melody's rise-and-fall, stepwise         |
| stripe spacing        | voicing in thirds — a planed 13th chord      |
| color saturation      | dynamics                                     |
| sun disc              | harp-gliss coda shimmer                      |
| left-to-right scene   | storm → jubilee → glow: the emotional arc    |

The catalog rhymes, too: The Window looks out at weather, Perigee falls
*through* the sky — Roy G. Biv is what the sky does after. And it is the
first piece in the catalog whose self-portrait is the deliverable: the roll
has always been the diagnostic; this time the diagnostic is the art.
