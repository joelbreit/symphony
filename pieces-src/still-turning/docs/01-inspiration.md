# Inspiration — "Still Turning"

## The image

Cambridge, late 1967. A graduate student named Jocelyn Bell is operating a
radio telescope she helped build — an array of wooden posts and wire strung
across four acres, designed to study the scintillation of quasars. It has no
screen. It has a chart recorder, and the chart recorder produces something on
the order of a hundred feet of paper a day, and she reads all of it by hand.

Somewhere in that paper she finds a quarter inch of what she called **a bit
of scruff**. Not a signal, not a source — just a smear in the trace where
there should have been nothing. It is small enough to ignore and she has
miles of paper to get through.

She does not ignore it. She goes back, finds it again, and notices it comes
back *on the sidereal day* — four minutes earlier every night, which is what
things fixed to the stars do and what things fixed to the Earth never do.
She runs the recorder faster to see inside the smear, and the smear turns out
to be a train of pulses **1.3373 seconds apart**, holding that period better
than any clock in the building.

For a while nobody can name a natural object that keeps time like that, and
the source gets the only half-joking label available: **LGM-1**. Little Green
Men.

It was not little green men. It was better. It was the collapsed core of a
dead star, a city-sized object of impossible density, spinning on its axis
and sweeping a beam past the Earth once every rotation. A lighthouse made
out of a star's corpse. Its designation is PSR B1919+21 and it has been
turning the entire time — through the announcement, through the 1974 Nobel
Prize that went to her supervisor and not to her, through every year of every
life that has happened since. It is turning right now, at that same period,
whether anyone is pointed at it or not.

**That is the piece.** Not "space is big." Two things at once:

- something that does not change, at all, ever; and
- a person who looked at a quarter inch of scruff and refused to let it go.

## Why a passacaglia

Because a passacaglia is the only form built out of exactly that.

A passacaglia is a bass line that repeats without alteration while everything
above it is rebuilt, statement after statement. The ground does not develop.
It does not modulate to accommodate you. Its indifference is the whole
engine: the variations get their meaning from being measured against
something that will not move.

Which is what a pulsar *is*. A pulsar is a passacaglia that has been running
for a million years. The astronomers' word for what Bell did — timing —
is our word too.

So the mapping is not a metaphor laid over the music. It is the same object:

| the sky | the score |
|---|---|
| the rotation period, invariant | the bar: one ground note, one pulse, 1.3373 s |
| the pulse train | a single high A struck on the downbeat — wherever two hands can reach it |
| the eight-note ground | eight bars — one full turn of the form |
| resolving finer structure in the signal | the subdivision ladder: quarters → eighths → triplets → sixteenths |
| "it might be a message" | the middle section, where the music phases against its own bar |
| "it's a neutron star" | the same bass, in the major |
| still turning | the last thing you hear is the pulse, alone, at the same rate |

The one row that turned out to have a catch is the pulse. I wanted it struck
in every bar of all 232 and it cannot be: a piano has two hands, and under
the theme statements the bass, the tune and a note at the top of the keyboard
are three hand positions. The first draft wrote it anyway, and the hand audit
called it — correctly — impossible about two hundred times.

What replaced it is better than what I planned. The pulse is explicit
wherever two hands can reach it, and where they cannot, the music is standing
on it: it is buried through the theme statements and the density ladder, it
comes back as the top note of every rolled chord in the two summits, and at
the end it is the only thing left. Losing it and finding it again is the
piece. A tick under everything for five minutes would only have been a
metronome — and worse, it would have made the point *about* attention while
demanding none.

## The one rule

**The tempo never changes after the opening.** No ritardando at the end.
No stretch into the big chord. Nothing.

Every instinct trained on Romantic piano writing wants to broaden the final
cadence, and the first draft of anything like this will do it automatically.
It has to be refused, because the entire subject of the piece is a thing that
does not slow down for us. The music can get denser, louder, higher, wider —
all the excitement has to come from those — but the clock is the star's, and
the star does not care that we have arrived at the good part.

The one tempo change in the piece is at the very start, and it is the
opposite gesture: the opening ticks at one pulse per *beat*, and when the
ground locks in, the tempo triples so that one pulse becomes one *bar*.
The pulse rate on the stopwatch is identical across that change. Nothing
sped up. We just started being able to see what was between the pulses.

## Why it should be inspiring rather than merely large

The cheap version of this piece is awe at the cosmos: big chords, wide
spacing, a slow build, done. The awe is real but it is not the story.

The story is that the universe was doing this the entire time and it took a
person willing to chase a quarter inch of scruff for anyone to know. The
grandeur belongs to the star; the *inspiration* belongs to the attention.
So the piece has to open small and quiet and slightly boring — one repeated
note that a listener could easily dismiss — and it has to end by revealing
that the eight bass notes everyone stopped hearing forty variations ago were
the melody all along.

If a listener comes away thinking "the little thing I keep noticing might be
enormous," the piece worked.

## Two facts I did not invent, and one I did

Checked, and used literally: the period 1.3373 s (which sets both tempos),
the sidereal drift, the "bit of scruff" and "LGM-1" language, the 1974 Nobel.

Invented: everything about what the music does with them. The star is not
in D minor. Nobody heard a tune. The tune is the part we bring.
