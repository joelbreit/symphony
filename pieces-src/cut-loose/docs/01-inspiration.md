# Inspiration — "Cut Loose"

## The image

New Orleans. A brass band walks a hearse from the church to the cemetery,
and the band is burying one of its own: the cornet player. Everyone who
ever played a job with him is in the front line — the clarinet, the alto,
the trombone, the sousaphone, the two drummers — and his chair is empty.
The snare drummer has loosened the snares; the drum makes a dull, wet
thud. They play the hymn at a walking pace, the one he always called on
the way out of a job, and the alto and trombone carry the tune because
there is nobody else to carry it.

At the grave the body is **cut loose** — the old phrase for the moment the
mourners let the dead go and the band turns for home. The grand marshal
blows his whistle. The snare drummer tightens the snares. The cadence
changes under everyone's feet, the sousaphone drops a riff, and the same
hymn comes back at three times the speed as a strut — and the cornet is
on top of it, first note of the day, as if he had only gone on ahead and
waited for the band to catch up. The umbrellas go up. The second line
dances all the way back to the neighborhood.

**One tune, two lives.** A jazz funeral is the only musical form whose
whole subject is that a melody can grieve and dance without changing a
note. That is the piece.

## The hook

The hymn's opening call — a rising sixth from the fifth of the key to the
third, then the fall back through the fourth and third:

    G · B♭ · E♭ · D · C · B♭    (in E♭: sol · te-less do-mi… no: 3̂ 5̂ 1̂ 7̂ 6̂ 5̂)

Slow, it is a hymn line — a breath, a reach, a settling. Fast and
syncopated, with the E♭ landing on the *and* of two and hanging there, it
is a strut. Every strain of the piece grows out of this call or answers
it: the sousaphone riff that starts the second line is the call in the
bass; the ramble strain's stop-time break quotes it; the final benediction
is the call, alone, unhurried, on the cornet.

## The cast

| Instrument | Character |
|---|---|
| Cornet | The one being buried. Silent for the whole procession; the first note after the whistle is his. Leads the second line like nothing happened. |
| Clarinet | The one who cries. Carries the hymn's second half with the widest vibrato in the band; in the second line, the filigree above everything. |
| Alto sax | The steady friend. States the hymn plainly, low and warm; in the second line, the riffing engine behind the lead. |
| Trombone | The old-timer. Smears into every downbeat of the dirge; tailgate in the second line; one chorus of his own on the ramble. |
| Sousaphone | The ground. Half notes on the walk out, two-beat home, four-beat when the umbrellas go up. |
| Snare drum | The grand marshal's clock. Muffled rolls on the way out; the whistle; the street beat home. |
| Bass drum & cymbal | The heartbeat. One and three in the dirge; the "boom — ba-boom" of the second line; the coat-hanger cymbal chick. |

Two drummers, not a kit: brass bands split the drums across two people
who have never in their lives played a ride pattern.

## The emotional arc

1. **The cadence** — muffled snare, bass drum, the walk begins.
2. **The hymn** (E♭) — alto and trombone, plain; clarinet joins above on
   the second half; the borrowed minor iv is the tear.
3. **The cry** — the last half again, clarinet leading at full voice, the
   trombone smearing under it, everyone louder than a dirge should be.
4. **Amen** — the plagal cadence, held, dying; the roll fades; silence.
5. **The whistle** — one long, two short. The snares come on. The street
   beat starts alone.
6. **The sousaphone riff** — the hymn's call as a bass line; the horns
   riff in.
7. **The strut** — the hymn, cut loose. The cornet enters. Collective
   improvisation.
8. **The ramble** (A♭) — a new hot strain with a stop-time break;
   trombone, then clarinet, take it.
9. **The umbrellas go up** — shout chorus, tempo pushing, drum breaks.
10. **Home** — the strut at full boil, sousaphone walking four, out chorus
    and the tag.
11. **Benediction** — after the last hit, the cornet alone plays the
    hymn's call, slow, with the bass drum answering once. He's still here.

## Why this one is mine

The catalog's two Dixieland pieces stand still: a band on a corner
(Royal Street Rattler) and a band at the end of a setlist (High Street
Riot). This one walks. It is also the piece in the catalog about the empty
chair — The Window was about a mind that closes, Perigee about a machine
that falls; this is about what a band does with a silence where a voice
used to be, which is to play his tune faster until he seems to be there.
That is the most generous idea in American music, and it is the whole
reason Dixieland exists.

## Working title

**Cut Loose** — subtitle: *a second line for a cornet player*.
