# Self-assessment — "Majority Rules"

## Measured against the blueprint

- **Duration and scale:** 4,180 notes, 660.5 beats, 3:43.7 of written music;
  229.7 seconds including the soundfont's tail. The planned 164-bar form and
  3.5–4.5 minute window held.
- **Roster:** exactly seven players — cornet, clarinet, alto, trombone,
  sousaphone, snare, bass drum — with no keyboard, banjo, or hidden kit.
- **Ranges:** all clean. Cornet F4–D6; clarinet F4–G6; alto G3–A♭5;
  trombone G2–B♭4; sousaphone F1–E♭3.
- **Audio:** peak −5.81 dBFS, zero clipped samples. The two designed valleys
  register: point of order −29.2 dBFS and table the motion −27.7, against
  −25.1 for the returning motion. The unanimous chorus is −23.0; the closing
  vote reaches −21.0. The last act is measurably the biggest.
- **Score:** MusicXML exported from the symbolic layer; synchronization gate
  reports 0 ms worst drift.

## What works

1. **It has two tunes, not one texture.** The motion is angular and public;
   the amendment begins with longer values and a gentler E♭ lift. Their first
   overlap at bar 37 is light (clarinet motion, sparse alto amendment); their
   final overlap at bar 141 is categorical (block-harmony motion, trombone
   amendment). The climax develops a relationship rather than only adding
   volume.
2. **The band members behave like characters.** Alto alone gets the lyrical
   proposal. Trombone revoices that proposal into tailgate range. Clarinet's
   sixteen-bar filibuster grows from rests and quarters into uninterrupted
   eighth-note speech. The four roll-call ballots share the same D–F–B♭ DNA
   but are authored for different registers and kinds of articulation.
3. **The formal jokes are audible events.** Point of order strips the texture
   to stop-time claims and drum answers; tabling removes every horn; recount
   has three differently contradicted final chords, then a nearly empty bar.
   These are changes in musical behavior, not labels pasted onto continuous
   backing.
4. **B♭ earns the last word.** E♭ and G minor are real regions, but the
   two-beat bass and dominant chains keep B♭ gravitational. The unison final
   hook spans five octaves and the sousaphone's last pitch is mechanically
   checked as B♭.

## Honest ledger

1. **The unanimous chorus is deliberately crowded.** Three-part block
   harmony, a moving trombone counterline, four-beat sousaphone, and the
   street cadence are the conceit; a live arranger might thin bars 9–12 to
   make the E♭-minor shadow speak more clearly.
2. **The amendment's first statement is low by design.** Alto at G3–E♭5
   distinguishes it from the cornet, but General MIDI can make that register
   less projecting than a real saxophonist would. If human listening finds it
   buried, the fix is orchestral (thin clarinet/trombone backgrounds), not a
   melody rewrite.
3. **The filibuster is written improvisation.** Its growing note density and
   chord-scale fit are controlled, but a real clarinet player would bend the
   phrase lengths and breathe less symmetrically.
4. **Shared idioms are intentionally reused.** The street beat, two-beat bass,
   riff engine, obbligato, and tailgate language come from *Cut Loose* because
   this is the same walking roster. The themes, civic form, gavel, ballots,
   counterpoint, and endings are new. That is the line between a returning
   band and a recycled song.

## Verdict

The piece is distinct where it matters: two authored strains, procedural
interruption as form, individual ballots, and a contrapuntal rather than
accumulative climax. It is obnoxious without becoming shapeless, and B♭ wins
by something closer to consensus than force — although force helps.
