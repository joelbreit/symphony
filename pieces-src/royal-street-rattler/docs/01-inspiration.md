# Inspiration — "Royal Street Rattler"

## The image

New Orleans, late afternoon, 1924. A streetcar rattles down Royal Street toward the river,
iron wheels clicking over the rail joints in a syncopated *ka-tak... ka-ta-TAK* that never
quite lands where you expect. On the corner of Royal and St. Peter, a six-piece street band
catches that rhythm out of the air — the cornet player grins, lifts his horn, and plays the
rattle back at the streetcar. By the second chorus, every pedestrian on the block has stopped
walking and started **strutting**.

That's the piece: **the moment a city's noise turns into its music.**

## The hook

The "rattle" — a repeated-note figure with an offbeat accent that imitates wheels hitting a
rail joint: three even eighth notes on one pitch, then a leap up landing on the *and* of the
beat and hanging there. Syncopation you can see in a streetcar's gait. Every strain of the
piece grows out of this cell or answers it.

## The cast (who's "speaking")

| Instrument | Character |
|---|---|
| Trumpet (cornet) | The bandleader — confident, declamatory, leads every ensemble |
| Clarinet | A kid weaving through the crowd — filigree above everything, never still |
| Trombone | The streetcar itself — tailgate smears, low swagger, a little menace in the minor strain |
| Tenor sax | A riverboat man leaning on a post — unhurried, bluesy, tells one good story |
| Sousaphone | The wheels — two-beat pulse, walks up the hills between phrases |
| Banjo | The clicking rails — relentless four-to-the-bar chunk |
| Drums | The city — woodblocks, press-roll snare, a streetcar bell (cowbell) in the intro |

## The emotional arc

1. **Bell & fanfare** — the streetcar approaches, the band wakes up
2. **The Strut** (F major) — pure joy, the catchy tune everyone whistles later
3. **Balcony Shadows** (D minor) — the car passes under the wrought-iron balconies; the
   trombone growls; swagger with a wink of menace, never actually dark
4. **Back into the sun** — the Strut returns
5. **Out to the river** (trio, B♭ major) — the street opens up; sunny, simpler tune;
   passengers take turns telling their stories (clarinet, sax, trumpet solos)
6. **Everybody dances** — drum break, two shout choruses, collective improvisation at
   full boil
7. **The tag** — the classic Dixieland three-break tag: trombone, clarinet, trumpet each
   get two beats of fame, then the whole band slams the door. One last sousaphone *plop*
   like the streetcar disappearing around the bend.

## Why this works for me

Dixieland is *conversation* music — three melodic voices improvising at once without
stepping on each other, over a rhythm section that never stops grinning. A composition
that's really a transcription of a conversation. The streetcar gives me a concrete
rhythmic identity (the hook), a route (the form), and a reason for the music to keep
moving forward the whole time.
