# Inspiration — "High Street Riot"

## The origin

The High Street Jazz Band — Morgantown, WV, college students, Dixieland instrumentation,
membership that swelled past what improv jazz can politely hold. When the setlist ran dry
they played "G Minor Jam": no melody, no chart, just the tuba laying down a lead and
everyone piling on until it got *angry*. Not mean-angry — the joyful kind of angry, the
kind a tuba-and-trombone-proficient horn section gets to feel when somebody finally says
*you may now wail*.

The transcription confirms it: a two-bar G pedal vamp with chromatic walk-ups (F–F♯→G),
a falling tail (D–C–B♭–B♮–C), horns stacking in thirds, a ♭5 snarl (D♭ over G), a break,
then a hammered tritone cluster. Accumulation → riot. That's the whole song. That's plenty.

## The image

It's 11:40pm at the end of a street gig. The band has played everything it knows twice.
Somebody shrugs. The sousaphone player looks left, looks right, and drops a riff so greasy
the streetlights flicker — and one by one, eight people who are *way* too many people for
this music decide they are each, personally, the lead. By the end the whole block is a riot
with a beat, and then — one wink from the tuba — it stops on a dime.

**The piece is the lifecycle of a vamp: invented → owned → fought over → detonated.**

## The hook (rule #1: drip with catchiness)

Everything hangs on THE RIFF — a two-bar sousaphone line in G minor:

- **Bar 1 (the strut up):** G … *(breath)* … G–B♭–C–C♯–D — root, rest, then a blues-third
  climb with a chromatic shove into the fifth.
- **Bar 2 (the snarl down):** D … *(breath)* … D♭!–C–B♭–C–F♯ — the ♭5 leans on the door,
  walks down, and the F♯ hooks chromatically back into the next G. The loop has no exit.
  That's the point.

Design rules borrowed from every riff that ever got stuck in a head: breath after the
downbeat (Seven Nation Army rule), call-and-answer inside itself (Haitian Fight Song rule),
chromatic wraparound so the loop feeds itself (boogie rule), singable by a person carrying
a couch (tuba rule).

## The cast

| Instrument | Character |
|---|---|
| Sousaphone | The leader. Invents the riff, never stops playing it, ends the argument |
| Trombone 1 & 2 | The muscle. Harmonize the riff, tailgate smears, start the fight |
| Cornet | The ego. Declamatory, thinks it's the lead. It is not. The tuba is |
| Clarinet | The shrieker. Commentary from above, increasingly unhinged |
| Tenor sax | The transfer student. Greasy, unbothered, naps until needed |
| Banjo | The floor. Four-to-the-bar chunk, does not blink |
| Drums | The crowd. Starts as a press roll, ends as a four-on-the-floor riot |

## The emotional arc

1. **The shrug** — sousa alone, riff born fully formed
2. **The pile-on** — drums, banjo, trombones in thirds: it's a band now
3. **The shout** — horns answer the riff in its rests: it's a SONG now
4. **The wail** — the anthem: long screamed descents, the angry-glory chorus
5. **The floor drops** — vamp alone again (you have to lose it to want it)
6. **The wailing order** — trombone, cornet, clarinet each take the lead; band can't
   help itself, keeps creeping back in behind them
7. **The argument** — trading twos that collapse into everyone-at-once polyphony
8. **The stomp** — stop-time, Neapolitan A♭ slams against G: maximum anger, maximum grin
9. **The riot** — riff up an octave, anthem on top, everything at once
10. **The wink** — dead stop. Sousa, alone, plays the snarl lick. Two stabs. One fat G. Done.
